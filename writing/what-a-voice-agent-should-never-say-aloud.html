What a voice agent should never say aloud

Six principles for designing voice and multimodal agents for people on the move.

November 2026

Most voice agents are designed as if the user is sitting at a desk, staring at a screen, with nothing else to do. Real people aren’t. They’re in a car park between appointments, walking into a shop, or carrying a box. Designing for voice starts with accepting that the ear is the most limited channel you have.

Decide what belongs to the ear and what belongs to the eye

Before writing a single line of dialogue, I sort every piece of information into four tiers: vital, important, helpful and hide. Only vital information earns a place in speech. Everything else waits for a screen, or doesn’t appear at all. I call this vital before visual.

Numbers that matter go to the screen

Prices, quantities and anything someone might need to check or compare should be shown, not spoken. People forget numbers they hear. They trust numbers they can see.

Anything that commits gets read back

If an action spends money, places an order or changes a record, the agent repeats it back and waits for a clear yes. Speed is good. Irreversible mistakes are not. See read back what commits.

Never more than three things in a turn

Spoken lists collapse after three items. If there are more, the agent offers the top three and asks whether the person wants the rest. See three per turn.

Design for the moment, not the feature

“What’s next?” and “What’s close by?” are better voice prompts than any menu. Someone driving between visits doesn’t need a dashboard. They need the agent to hold the thought until they’re ready.

Refusals are part of the experience

A good voice agent knows what it shouldn’t say aloud, whether that’s sensitive data, pricing in a public place, or a guess presented as fact. Designing those boundaries is the work, not an afterthought.

None of this is about the model. The difference between a voice agent people tolerate and one they rely on is the design around it: what it says, what it shows, and what it deliberately leaves unsaid.
