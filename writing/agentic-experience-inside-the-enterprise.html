What agentic experience looks like inside the enterprise

The journey doesn’t disappear when agents arrive. It moves into decisions someone has to design.

November 2026

This year’s Design in Tech Report names the shift many of us are already living through: from user experience to agentic experience, or UX to AX. John Maeda’s argument is that agents remove the journey, taking people straight to their goal, and that the cost of that convenience moves into tokens, trust, control and human agency.

I agree with the diagnosis. From inside enterprise programmes, though, the picture is slightly different. The journey doesn’t disappear. It moves inside the agent, into a series of decisions about what to say, what to show, what to do and what to refuse. Somebody has to design those decisions. If nobody does, the model’s defaults design them for you.

Trust is built in small moments

Enterprises don’t lose trust in an agent all at once. They lose it in moments: a confident guess in public, an order placed on a misheard word, a suggestion with no reason behind it. The fixes are rarely about the model. They’re design decisions: designed refusals, reading back anything that commits, and explaining every suggestion.

Control comes from structure, not settings

Giving people control over an agent usually means a settings page nobody visits. In practice, control comes from the shape of the conversation: answers from approved sources with depth on demand, and structure inside the conversation when precision matters. People stay in charge because the experience keeps the important choices in front of them.

Agency includes everyone

An agent that works brilliantly for most people and fails a few hasn’t preserved human agency. It has redistributed it. That’s why every mode, one experience sits alongside the others: voice, text, touch and sign, each able to complete the task.

Experience first, still

For fifteen years I’ve argued that enterprise technology is built the wrong way round: technology, then value, then experience as an afterthought. Agents make that order more dangerous, because the experience is now the product’s behaviour, not its surface. Define the experience first, use technology to close the gaps, and the value follows.

I’m collecting the patterns that make this work in an open library of agentic experience patterns. They come from live delivery, and they’ll keep growing.
